A first-five-minute journey

These are testing hypotheses, not a timer or a universal attention limit. Readers control pace and explanations never vanish after a fixed number of seconds.

Opening: “Try one simple hand.” Show cards and one concrete promise: explanations stay available.

First decision: Teach a pair beside the cards. Two answer choices plus “Show me first.” Avoid abbreviations, pot percentages and hand scores.

First explanation: Outline the relevant pair only after commitment. State the rule in one or two sentences. Let the learner control Next.

Changed example: Compare two pairs. Say which feature matters. Later test the first rule with a genuinely new deal; a second rule is not proof of transfer.

Small checkpoint: “2 examples explored.” Show the two rules and offer the remaining 3 comparisons or a comfortable stop. Exposure is not mastery.

Optional continuation: A short hand together after basics, or a chosen lesson. Ask familiarity only where it helps this choice. Keep the existing chart available and help neutral.

Success, mistakes and uncertainty

What the research supports—and what it does not

Primary experiments, original reviews and Apple guidance were checked. Most studies concern school, memory or everyday habits. Applying them to casual poker learning is a design inference, not a measured retention result.

Confidence comes from a comprehensible action

A synthesis of 144 studies found competence was the strongest predictor of self-determined student motivation. Bureau et al.,2022.

Apply: Offer one attainable decision, then explain the exact winning feature. Keep an experienced-user exit.

Limit: Educational evidence; no causal onboarding or retention result for Glass Table.

Show the rule before asking

Five algebra experiments studied worked examples as an alternative to unguided novice problem solving. Sweller & Cooper,1985.

Apply: First explain two equal ranks as a pair; only then ask which hand wins. Cards carry the explanation.

Limit: Algebra experiments; experts may need less instruction.

A choice can teach; a guess is not mastery

Retrieval improved delayed retention of prose, although repeated study performed better immediately. Roediger & Karpicke,2006.

Apply: After a taught example, use a changed deal. Explain correct answers too. Reserve mastery claims for stronger evidence.

Limit: Memory tasks, not mobile engagement; a single multiple-choice answer is weak evidence.

Feedback needs useful information

A feedback meta-analysis included435 studies and found differing effects across feedback types. Wisniewski et al.,2020.

Apply: Name what to look at and why it wins. Give a next action. Wrong and unsure are different states.

Limit: Learning feedback is heterogeneous; generic praise is not equivalent to explanation.

Make progress visible and accurate

Across138 randomized studies, monitoring progress improved goal attainment on average (d=.40). Harkin et al.,2016.

Apply: Show2 examples completed and the rules explored; later show comparable practice counts and a small activity graphic.

Limit: Goal attainment across domains, not proof that a streak increases app retention.

Use praise that earns trust

Praise can help, hurt or have no effect; sincerity, autonomy, competence and social comparison matter. Henderlong & Lepper,2002.

Apply: Use “That’s right—the pair decides this hand.” Avoid identity labels and praise for inferred effort.

Limit: Primarily child research; adult Korean/English tone must be tested.

Badges are not a substitute for a useful lesson

A five-week study-app investigation found greater autonomous motivation with gamification, but not greater app-use duration/frequency or performance. Baars et al.,2022.

Apply: Make the practice satisfying first; keep the already-approved quiet activity indicator optional and secondary.

Limit: One student app/context; not a general verdict against all gamification.

Returning should feel easy

A study of96 volunteers repeating everyday behaviors found substantial variation in habit formation; one missed opportunity did not materially change the process. Lally et al.,2010.

Apply: Offer a small repeatable practice and kind re-entry. Keep earned progress when people miss days.

Limit: Food/drink/activity behaviors; no universal21-day app habit promise.

Protect the thought in progress

Phone notifications impaired an attention-demanding laboratory task even without interacting with the phone. Stothart et al.,2015.

Apply: Keep the explanation present until Next. Avoid reward interruptions, auto-advance and notification prompts in the opening.

Limit: Does not measure a universal human attention span.

Teach through the interface

Apple recommends onboarding that is fast, fun and optional, with instruction beside what it describes. Apple onboarding.

Apply: A small card comparison, visible help and a clear exit fit the product better than a long introductory speech.

Limit: Platform guidance; not psychological outcome evidence.

Further mechanism context: mastery experiences supported efficacy in a clinical adult experiment, which is far from poker onboarding. It suggests testing an earned first action rather than relying on praise alone. Bandura et al.,1977. Full research notes and source boundaries.

  • One-tap answers, available help and optional chart.
  • Bottom actions and visible Previous/Close.
  • Quiet activity indicator, facts beside the recap, and professional tone.

Refine the meaning

  • Separate “explored,” “completed,” and “recalled in a new example.”
  • Show a compact lesson path and actual activity dots, not just text blocks.
  • Use improvement claims only with comparable concepts, denominators and enough evidence.
  • After repeated trouble, offer a simpler example or demonstration. After a break, offer a small next task.

Your strongest product decisions are help freedom, simple input and wanting to explain every decision. How to decide which opening works

Start with small observed usability rounds:4–6 adult poker beginners per language, plus a few people who already play. This is a practical recruitment target, not a statistically powered retention experiment. Give each person only one opening first; alternate A/B between participants, then use the other for preference discussion. C can be removed unless it solves an observed issue.

  1. Can they begin without your explanation? Record hesitations, terms they cannot explain and time to a useful action. Faster is not automatically better.
  2. Did they learn the rule? Ask them to explain why the hand wins in their own words, then give a new deal with changed ranks/ownership. Recheck after24–48 hours if they consent.
  3. Are wrong and unsure safe? Observe both states. Does the learner know what to inspect and how to continue? Never treat choosing help as failure.
  4. Can they stop and return? Find the exit, notice what progress actually means, and choose their own next step. Use voluntary diaries/interviews for return motivation.
  5. Can they use the controls? Test12 mini, normal/large text, both languages/appearances, one-handed taps, screen readers and Reduce Motion on native implementation.

Keep researcher notes with consent rather than adding telemetry to the offline app. Measure explanation and delayed transfer alongside enjoyment and voluntary return; session length, a streak and fast guesses do not prove learning. For a later quantitative comparison, calculate sample size from a defined outcome and observed baseline instead of pretending a small round proves a retention lift.
